You're behind on rent. Maybe it's one week, maybe it's a month and climbing, and the messages from the property manager have changed tone.

Here's the thing about this situation: Tenancy Services documents the arrears process in detail — but it's written for landlords. How to issue the notice. Which form to use. When you can apply to the Tribunal. Search it from the other side and you get the mechanics of your own eviction with none of the timing that actually matters to you.

So this is the tenant's version. Same law, same timeframes, read from where you're standing.

**The single most important thing to know first:** your landlord cannot evict you. Only the [Tenancy Tribunal](https://www.justice.govt.nz/tribunals/tenancy-tribunal/) can end your tenancy, and only after a hearing. Locks changed, belongings on the lawn, "be out by Sunday" — all unlawful, no matter what you owe.

## The Actual Timeline

Per [Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/rent-bond-and-bills/rent/overdue-rent/), here's what can happen and when.

| Stage | Trigger | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Notice of Overdue Rent** | Rent unpaid for at least **5 working days** | A formal warning. Each occasion rent goes 5 working days unpaid counts separately |
| **14-day notice to remedy** | You owe **less than 21 days' rent** | 14 calendar days to clear the arrears *and* keep paying current rent |
| **Tribunal application** | **3 separate occasions** in a **90-day period** | Landlord applies under **s55(1)(aa)** to end the tenancy |
| **The deadline on them** | Application must be filed **within 28 days** of the third notice | A hard date on their side — check it |

Two details in that table are worth slowing down on, because they cut your way.

**Five working days, not one.** Rent being late on the due date isn't yet an arrears event in the legal sense. The clock starts at five working days — and working days exclude weekends and public holidays. A payment that lands on day four is late, but it doesn't feed the three-notice count.

**The three notices have to be within 90 days of each other.** They don't accumulate forever. A notice from five months ago isn't part of a current sequence.

## The 28-Day Deadline Nobody Tells Tenants About

This one is genuinely useful and almost never mentioned in tenant-facing content.

Once the third notice has been given, the landlord has to file the Tribunal application **within 28 days**. Not eventually. Not whenever they get around to it.

Landlords and property managers miss administrative deadlines like anyone else. If you're at the sharp end of this process, it's worth knowing exactly when the third notice was dated — and if an application arrives well outside that window, raising the timing is a fair question to put to the Tribunal.

> **Key point:** Write down the date on every notice you receive, the moment you receive it. Photograph it. The dates are what the whole process runs on, and they're the first thing that gets fuzzy under stress.

## What Makes a Notice Defective

Tenancy Services is explicit that you can challenge a notice on two grounds:

1. **The rent wasn't 5 working days overdue** when the notice was issued
2. **The notice didn't meet the legal requirements**

On the second — each notice has to state the amount overdue, the dates, **your right to challenge it**, and **how many prior notices have been given**. A notice missing those elements is arguably defective.

This is not a technicality to hide behind if you genuinely owe months of rent. But it is a real defence, it's underused, and a sequence of three notices where one is defective is not a sequence of three valid notices.

If you think a notice is wrong, you can apply to the Tribunal yourself to challenge it rather than waiting to be taken there. Our [Tribunal application guide](/posts/tenancy-tribunal-how-to-apply-nz/) covers how, and there's a [shorter brief version](/briefs/tenancy-tribunal-application-nz-brief/) if you want the quick path.

## What To Do This Week

In rough order of how much they help:

**Talk to them before they talk to the Tribunal.** An arrangement agreed in writing — even a modest one — changes the picture entirely. A landlord who has accepted $80 a week towards arrears is in a much weaker position at a hearing than one who was ignored. Put any agreement in writing, and keep paying the current rent on top of it, because a repayment deal that lets current rent slide just rebuilds the problem.

**Get the money help you might not know you qualify for.** Two Work and Income payments matter here, and **you don't have to be on a benefit for either**:

- The [Accommodation Supplement](https://www.workandincome.govt.nz/products/a-z-benefits/accommodation-supplement.html) — a weekly payment towards rent, board or home ownership costs, for ongoing trouble paying. A lot of working renters never check whether they qualify.
- The [Accommodation Costs Arrears Grant](https://www.workandincome.govt.nz/housing/live-in-home/housing-costs/accommodation-costs-arrears.html) — for overdue rent where you may be evicted because of it. **This one has to be paid back**, with a repayment plan set to suit your circumstances, so it's a way to stop the eviction clock rather than free money. It covers up to **4 weeks** of accommodation costs, you can generally only get it **twice in 12 months**, and income and asset limits apply.

If the underlying problem is your bills rather than the rent, our [winter power bills guide](/posts/power-bills-nz-rental-winter-2026/) covers the hardship protections and the Winter Energy Payment.

**Get free advice, early.** [Community Law](https://communitylaw.org.nz/) and [Citizens Advice Bureau](https://www.cab.org.nz/) both deal with this constantly and neither charges you. The options are widest before an application is filed and narrow steadily afterwards.

**Turn up to the hearing.** If it does reach the Tribunal, attending matters more than almost anything else you can do. An adjudicator who hears your circumstances, sees a payment record, and is handed a realistic repayment proposal is in a completely different position from one who hears only the landlord because you didn't come.

## The Bit That Follows You: Tribunal Orders Are Public

Worth knowing before things get that far, because it changes the maths on settling early.

Tenancy Tribunal orders are **published and searchable online**. The Ministry of Justice runs a [public search of Tribunal orders](https://forms.justice.govt.nz/search/TT/) — searchable by landlord or tenant name, property address, or application number — and per [Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/disputes/searching-tribunal-orders/) most orders are available online for **at least 3 years**. Older ones can still be obtained on paper from the District Court that heard the dispute, for a small fee.

So an order against you for rent arrears sits in a free database anyone can search — including the next landlord assessing your application.

Two qualifications worth stating fairly. **Not every order is published** — some are withheld for legal or technical reasons. And names and identifying details are removed from some published orders.

There's also something actionable in that second point: **a party who was substantially successful in their claim can request a suppression order** to have their personal information removed from the published decision. So if you go to a hearing and win, ask about it. Most people don't know it's available.

Short of that, plan on the basis that an order will be visible.

This is the strongest practical argument for resolving arrears before a hearing rather than after one. A repayment arrangement agreed directly with your landlord leaves no public record. An order does, and it lands in exactly the place your next application gets assessed.

If you want to see what this looks like from the other direction, our guide on [how to check a landlord](/posts/how-to-check-a-landlord-nz/) uses the same database — it works both ways.

## If You Think the Arrears Aren't Real

It happens more than you'd expect — payments credited to the wrong account, a rent increase applied without valid notice, bond wrongly treated as rent, water charges added incorrectly.

Before you accept the number, check it:

- [ ] **Your own bank records** for every payment, against their ledger
- [ ] **Any rent increase** — was it once in 12 months with 60 days' written notice? See [can my landlord raise the rent mid-tenancy](/briefs/can-landlord-increase-rent-mid-tenancy-nz/)
- [ ] **Water charges** — [what a landlord can actually charge for](/briefs/can-landlord-charge-for-water-nz/)
- [ ] **Bond** — it isn't rent, and can't be used as rent during the tenancy. See [your bond rights](/posts/your-bond-rights-nz/)

Ask for a full ledger in writing. A landlord who can't produce one has a problem at a hearing.

## One Thing Worth Naming

If the arrears started *after* you complained about something — a repair, an inspection, a healthy homes issue — the timing matters. Retaliatory action is unlawful in New Zealand and the Tribunal takes it seriously. Our guide to [retaliatory termination](/posts/retaliatory-termination-nz/) covers what that looks like and how it's proved.

That's a narrower situation than most, and it isn't a defence to genuinely unpaid rent. But if it fits, raise it.

## The Timeline, Summarised

- Rent has to be **5 working days** overdue before a notice can issue
- A **14-day notice to remedy** applies where you owe less than 21 days' rent
- **Three notices in 90 days** opens the door to a Tribunal application under s55(1)(aa)
- The landlord must file **within 28 days** of the third notice
- Only the **Tribunal** can end your tenancy — never your landlord
- **Defective notices** and miscounted arrears are real, checkable defences
- **Turn up** to the hearing

Being behind on rent is not the end of a tenancy. It's the start of a process with defined steps and defined dates, most of which give you room to act. Use them early — that's the whole difference.
