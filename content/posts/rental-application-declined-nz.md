Fourth application this month. Same result, same non-answer: *"the owner has decided to go in another direction."*

Nobody tells you why. And in the absence of a reason, most people default to assuming the worst thing about themselves — my income's too low, my job's too casual, they didn't like me at the viewing — and then start over-sharing on the next application to compensate.

That instinct is the problem. Handing over more personal information than you need to doesn't strengthen an application, and quite a lot of what gets asked for shouldn't be asked for in the first place.

So let's do two things: what a landlord in New Zealand can actually require of you, and what genuinely moves the needle.

---

## What They're Allowed to Ask For

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner publishes [rental guidance for tenants](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/rental-guidance-for-tenants/), and it's more protective than most renters realise. Three rules matter.

### 1. Credit and criminal checks are stage-gated

A landlord can ask for your **consent** to a credit or criminal check on the application form. But the check itself should only be carried out **once they're actively negotiating an offer of tenancy with you**.

Not on every applicant. Not at the initial sift. At the point where you're the one they're talking to about actually renting the place.

If you're being credit checked as one of forty applicants at an open home, that's not what the guidance contemplates — and you're entitled to ask at what stage they intend to run it.

### 2. Your spending is off limits

The guidance is blunt on this: landlords should never ask about "your spending habits (e.g. bank statements showing transactions)."

Not "should think carefully about." Should never.

There's one narrow exception — if you later fall behind and are negotiating a repayment plan, it may become reasonable for them to look at your expenses. That's a different situation from applying for a house. We've covered this in more detail in [can a landlord ask for bank statements](/briefs/can-landlord-ask-for-bank-statements-nz/).

### 3. You choose how you prove you can pay

Beyond a credit report, a landlord can ask for **one other form of evidence** that you can afford the rent. The options include:

- A pay slip
- A letter from your employer
- Work and Income documentation
- Evidence of rental payments in a previous tenancy

And here's the line worth memorising, straight from the guidance: *"You should be able to choose which form of evidence you will provide."*

One other form. Your choice which.

> **Key point:** If an application form demands three months of bank statements, your employment contract, and a credit check up front, that's not a thorough landlord — it's a request for considerably more than the Privacy Commissioner's guidance supports. You can provide the evidence you're comfortable with and say so.

### If you're asked for too much

Raise it with the landlord or property manager first — often it's a form template nobody's reviewed in years rather than anything deliberate. If that doesn't resolve it, you can complain to the [Privacy Commissioner](https://www.privacy.org.nz/), but be aware the complaint needs to show you **suffered harm** as a result, which is a real threshold.

## What Actually Sinks Applications

Now the other half. Setting aside the market being brutal — which it is, and which isn't about you — here's what tends to make the difference between two similar applicants.

**Incompleteness.** Applications are frequently assessed in the order they arrive, with whatever's attached. A complete application at 9pm on viewing day beats a stronger candidate's half-finished one on Thursday. Missing referee phone numbers are the single most common gap.

**Referees who don't answer.** A property manager working through a shortlist will try your referee twice. If nobody picks up, you drop down the list — not because of anything about you, but because they can't verify you and someone else is already verified. **Tell your referees they're listed, and check they'll actually answer an unknown number.**

**No previous landlord.** If you've never rented, or rented overseas, or only ever been a flatmate who wasn't on the agreement, you have a gap where the most useful reference should be. Our guides on [renting without NZ references](/posts/renting-without-nz-references-auckland/) and [first-time renting in Auckland](/posts/first-time-renting-in-auckland/) both deal with how to fill it.

**Slowness.** In a competitive market the gap between "applied that evening" and "applied two days later" is often decisive, and it has nothing to do with the quality of either application.

**Vagueness about who's moving in.** Landlords need to know how many people and who. An application that's unclear on this reads as a risk.

## Building an Application That Gets Through

Assemble this once, keep it in a folder, and reuse it:

- [ ] **Photo ID**
- [ ] **Your chosen proof of income** — one form, the one you're most comfortable with
- [ ] **Two referees, briefed** — ideally a previous landlord or property manager plus one character referee
- [ ] **A written reference** from a previous landlord if you can get one — this is the highest-value item on the list and almost nobody has one
- [ ] **A short cover note** — who you are, who's moving in, when you can move, why this place
- [ ] **Everyone's details** for every adult moving in

The written landlord reference is the one worth going out of your way for. Ask your current or most recent landlord for one *before* you need it — most will write a few lines, and it converts an unreachable phone number into something a property manager can read in ten seconds.

The cover note doesn't need to be charming. Three sentences of clear facts beats a paragraph about how much you loved the kitchen.

## Property Manager or Private Landlord? It Changes the Game

Who's assessing you tends to affect what works, and most applicants pitch the same way to both.

**A property manager** is usually working a queue against a checklist, often across several properties in a week, and managing risk on someone else's behalf. What tends to land: completeness, verifiable referees, fast responses, nothing that creates extra work. What tends not to: personality, a long letter, anything needing a follow-up call to clarify.

**A private landlord** is choosing a person to put in a house they often have some attachment to. There's usually more room for context and someone with the discretion to weigh it. What tends to land: a short honest note about who you are, the reason for any gap in your history, and turning up to the viewing having actually read the listing.

The practical upshot: if you have a complication — no NZ credit file, a patchy rental history, self-employment, a break in work — a private landlord is often the better odds, because there's someone with the discretion to hear the explanation. A managed application usually has nowhere for context to go.

Our guide to [property manager versus private landlord](/posts/property-manager-vs-private-landlord/) covers the wider differences, including what each means once you're actually in the tenancy.

Worth knowing before the viewing, too: what you ask says something about you. [Questions to ask at a rental viewing](/posts/questions-to-ask-at-a-rental-viewing-nz/) has the list, and asking two good ones lands better than asking none.

## A Note on What You Shouldn't Have to Do

There's a version of rental application advice that amounts to: offer more rent, pay six months up front, write a letter about how much you want it.

Some of that is bad tactics, and some of it runs into hard limits. It is [unlawful for a landlord to ask for more than 2 weeks' rent in advance](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/rent-bond-and-bills/rent/charging-rent/), and a [general bond is capped at 4 weeks' rent](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/rent-bond-and-bills/bond/charging-a-bond/charging-a-general-bond/). So an offer of six months up front isn't the clever workaround it looks like — it's outside what a landlord is permitted to require.

If you're being asked to exceed either limit, that's worth knowing before you scrape the money together.

## If You Think It's Discrimination

Everything above assumes ordinary, if frustrating, market competition. If you have specific reason to believe you're being declined on a prohibited ground — race, national origin, family status, disability, receiving a benefit — that's a different matter with a different remedy path. Our brief on [discrimination in rental applications](/briefs/rental-application-discrimination-nz-brief/) covers what the law prohibits and what can be done.

The honest caveat: a series of unexplained declines is difficult to build a case from, because there's nothing on the record to point at. What makes a case is a pattern plus something in writing — so if something was said or sent that shouldn't have been, keep it.

## What You Can Actually Control

You can't control the market and you usually can't find out why you were declined. You can control:

- **What you hand over** — one form of income evidence, your choice, no spending history
- **When checks happen** — at offer stage, not at the sift
- **Completeness** — every field, every phone number
- **Referees** — briefed, reachable, ideally with something in writing
- **Speed** — same day, every time

Most declined applications aren't a verdict on you. They're a queue you weren't at the front of. Get the folder ready and get to the front.
