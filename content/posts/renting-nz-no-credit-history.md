You've landed in New Zealand with a job, savings, and a solid rental history somewhere else entirely. Then the application form asks for a credit check, and you realise the file it's going to pull is empty. Not bad — empty. You don't exist here yet.

It's a genuinely difficult position, and the advice you'll get from other renters is usually some version of *offer more money*. Most of that advice is wrong, and some of it describes something a landlord isn't permitted to accept.

Here's what actually works, and what the rules are.

---

## A Credit Report Is One Option, Not a Requirement

This is the reframe that matters.

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner's [rental guidance for tenants](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/rental-guidance-for-tenants/) sets out what a landlord may collect to confirm you can pay the rent. Beyond a credit report, they can ask for **one other form of evidence**, such as:

- A pay slip
- A letter from your employer
- Work and Income documentation
- Evidence of rental payments in a previous tenancy

And then this line, which is the whole article: *"You should be able to choose which form of evidence you will provide."*

You choose. So if the credit file is the weak link, don't lead with it — lead with the pay slip or the employment letter, and say so plainly.

> **Template:**
>
> > Hi [Name],
> >
> > I've recently moved to New Zealand, so I don't have a local credit history yet — it'll come back thin simply because I'm new here rather than for any other reason.
> >
> > What I can provide instead is [a letter from my employer confirming my role and salary / my most recent pay slips / records of rent paid at my last address, with a reference from that landlord].
> >
> > Happy to supply whichever is most useful to you.
> >
> > Cheers,
> > [Your name]

Naming it up front works better than hoping nobody notices. An empty credit file with no explanation reads as a red flag; an empty credit file with a clear reason and an alternative attached reads as an organised applicant.

## When They Can Run the Check At All

Worth knowing even if you're comfortable being checked: the Privacy Commissioner's guidance says credit and criminal checks should only be carried out **once the landlord is actively negotiating an offer of tenancy with you** — not on every applicant at the initial stage.

So being credit checked as one of thirty people at an open home isn't what the guidance contemplates. You can ask at what stage they intend to run it.

---

## The Limits on What You Can Be Asked to Pay

These limits aren't obvious if you've come from a market with different rules — and not knowing them is what makes a new arrival easy to over-charge.

| What | Legal maximum in NZ |
|---|---|
| General bond | **4 weeks' rent** |
| Pet bond (if applicable) | A further **2 weeks' rent** |
| Rent in advance | **2 weeks — it is unlawful to ask for more** |

Two things follow from that table.

**Offering six months up front is not a clever solution.** It's specifically outside what a landlord is permitted to ask for. A landlord also [can't ask for the next rent payment until all the rent already paid has been used up](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/rent-bond-and-bills/rent/charging-rent/) — so large prepayments don't work the way people assume.

**Your bond doesn't stay with the landlord.** It's lodged with Tenancy Services. If someone wants to hold it themselves, that's a warning sign about everything else in the arrangement. Our guide to [your bond rights](/posts/your-bond-rights-nz/) covers lodgement and the timeframes.

> **Warning:** If you're being asked for a large sum up front to compensate for having no credit history, that isn't a workaround — it's a request that exceeds what the rules allow. You can say so, and point at the limits above.

## What to Assemble Instead

Build the file before you start applying:

- [ ] **A letter from your employer** — role, start date, salary, on letterhead. The single most useful document you can hold
- [ ] **Recent pay slips** — two or three
- [ ] **A written reference from your previous landlord**, wherever they are. Get this *before* you leave the country if you can
- [ ] **Evidence of rent paid** — bank statements showing regular rent going out at your old address, or a copy of the previous tenancy agreement
- [ ] **Photo ID and visa or residency documentation** as applicable
- [ ] **Two contactable referees**, briefed, who will answer an unknown number

The overseas landlord reference is the highest-value item and the one most people don't get. Asking your current landlord for a few written lines while you're still their tenant takes one email. Finding them from another hemisphere eight months later is a different exercise entirely.

## A Note on Guarantors

With no credit file, being asked for a guarantor is common. It's a legitimate arrangement and it often does unlock a tenancy.

Two practical things, though. Whoever signs is agreeing to cover obligations you don't meet — so both of you want to read exactly what the guarantee document says before anyone signs it, and if the wording is open-ended, that's a conversation to have with [Community Law](https://communitylaw.org.nz/) rather than a form to sign quickly.

And if you're renting with others, make sure your guarantor understands the exposure: tenants who sign a tenancy agreement are jointly responsible for **all** the rent and any damage, [not just their own share](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/starting-a-tenancy/flatting/). A guarantor backing you in a five-bedroom flat is not backing one-fifth of the rent.

One more: an overseas guarantor is harder for a landlord to rely on than a local one — which is exactly the bind new arrivals are in, and why the employer letter usually does more work.

## What They Still Can't Ask For

Having no credit history does not lower the floor on your privacy.

Landlords should **never** ask about your spending habits, including bank statements showing transactions. That doesn't change because your file is thin — if anything it's the moment the request is most likely to appear. Our brief on [whether a landlord can ask for bank statements](/briefs/can-landlord-ask-for-bank-statements-nz/) covers the detail.

Providing evidence you can *afford* the rent is fair. Providing a transaction-by-transaction account of what you spend it on is not the same thing, and you can decline the second while offering the first.

### If you're asked for more than that

The [Privacy Commissioner's guidance](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/rental-guidance-for-tenants/) sets out the path, and the first step is less confrontational than people expect: *"If you're concerned about the questions a landlord or property manager is asking, try raising this concern with them first."*

You can ask why a particular piece of information is needed. Often the honest answer is that nobody has reviewed the application form in years, and the request quietly goes away.

If it doesn't resolve, you can complain to the Privacy Commissioner — but know the threshold before you invest in it: the complaint needs to demonstrate that **you suffered harm** as a result. That's a real bar, and it's worth knowing up front rather than discovering halfway through.

## Bring These Instead

- A credit report is **one option among several** — you can choose which evidence you provide
- **Name the gap up front** and attach the alternative
- Checks should happen at **offer stage**, not at the initial sift
- **2 weeks' rent in advance is the legal maximum** — large prepayments aren't a workaround
- **Bond is capped at 4 weeks** and goes to Tenancy Services, not the landlord
- **Spending history is still off limits**, thin credit file or not
- Get the **employer letter and the overseas landlord reference** before you need them

Having no credit history in New Zealand isn't a mark against you. It's an absence of data, and there's a defined list of things you're allowed to put in its place. Bring those instead, and bring them first.
