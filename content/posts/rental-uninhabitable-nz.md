Water is coming through the ceiling, or the storm took half the roof, or the fire brigade has just told you not to go back inside. Whatever brought you here, the question is the same and it's urgent: **do I still have to pay rent, and can I leave?**

New Zealand law has a clear answer to both, and it's more generous to tenants than most people expect. It lives in [section 59 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1986](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1986/0120/latest/DLM95528.html), which almost nobody reads until the day they need it.

Here it is in plain English.

---

## The Rent Stops for What You Can't Live In

Section 59 says that where premises are destroyed, or so seriously damaged as to be uninhabitable, **the rent abates**. "Abates" is old legal language for stops or reduces — and it's a consequence of the property's condition, not a favour your landlord grants you.

The word doing the work is *uninhabitable*. Not inconvenient. Not unpleasant. Not livable-but-annoying. The place has to be genuinely unfit to live in.

[Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/maintenance-and-inspections/damage-and-repairs/what-to-do-after-a-natural-disaster/) puts the practical version plainly: where you're required to vacate while repairs happen, you **won't have to pay rent until the property can be lived in again**.

If only part of the property is affected, the rent reduces accordingly — because, as Tenancy Services frames it, you no longer have enjoyment of the entire property. One unusable bedroom in a three-bedroom house doesn't stop the rent, it lowers it. There's no formula in the Act for what proportion is right, which means it's a negotiation, and a Tribunal decision if the negotiation fails.

> **Key point:** Abatement is not the same as withholding rent in protest. One is a legal consequence of the property's condition; the other is a breach of your agreement that can get you a 14-day notice to remedy. Get the reduction agreed in writing, and if your landlord won't engage, apply to the Tribunal rather than simply stopping payment.

## You Can Leave on Two Days' Notice

This is the part that surprises people. Under section 59, where the premises are uninhabitable:

| Who's ending it | Notice required |
|---|---|
| **Tenant** | **2 days** |
| **Landlord** | **7 days** |

Two days. And critically, **this applies to fixed-term tenancies as well as periodic ones**. A fixed term does not trap you in a house you can't live in — which is exactly the fear that keeps people paying rent on a flooded flat while they sleep at their sister's place.

Where only part of the place is damaged, either party can apply to the [Tenancy Tribunal](https://www.justice.govt.nz/tribunals/tenancy-tribunal/) for an order ending the tenancy. The Tribunal can grant it if it would be unreasonable to require the landlord to reinstate the property, or unreasonable to require you to carry on living there even at a reduced rent.

If you're heading down that road, our guide to [applying to the Tenancy Tribunal](/posts/tenancy-tribunal-how-to-apply-nz/) covers the process and the current fee.

## Proving It's Uninhabitable

You don't get to simply declare a house unlivable, and neither does your landlord get to declare it fine. [Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/maintenance-and-inspections/damage-and-repairs/what-to-do-after-a-natural-disaster/) says proof can take the form of a **notice from emergency services or council officials** — a placard, a fire service report, a council notice — and that **photos of the damage may be used by either party**, and provided to the Tenancy Tribunal if it's disputed.

That official notice is the strongest evidence available and it's usually created for you in the first 24 hours after a serious event. Keep it.

Build the rest of the record yourself, immediately:

- [ ] **Photos and video** of every affected room, dated, before anything is cleaned or moved
- [ ] **The official notice** — placard, council letter, fire service report
- [ ] **Written notification to your landlord**, with the time you sent it — text and email both, so there's a timestamp
- [ ] **Somewhere to sleep**, and receipts if you're paying for it
- [ ] **An inventory of damaged belongings**, photographed

Do this on day one. Memories fade, water dries, and landlords rebuild — and every one of those makes your position harder to prove three months later.

## Urgent Repairs: When You Can Fix It and Send the Bill

Short of total uninhabitability, there's a middle ground the law also handles. [Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/maintenance-and-inspections/repairs-and-damages/) treats repairs as **urgent** where "the state of disrepair is likely to cause injury to people or property."

Burst pipe. Blocked drain backing up. Electrical fault. A roof letting water into a bedroom.

For those, you can arrange the work yourself, and the landlord "must also pay the tenant back for any urgent repair work the tenant had to have done" — **provided you made reasonable attempts to let them know first**.

That proviso does a lot of work. Two conditions:

1. **It has to be genuinely urgent.** Injury to people or property, not a dripping tap you're sick of.
2. **You have to try to reach them first.** Reasonable attempts — plural. Call, text, and email, and keep the record of all three. A landlord who didn't answer at 11pm on a Saturday is a very different story from a landlord you never contacted.

Keep the work proportionate to the emergency, and keep every invoice — a plumber stopping a burst pipe is straightforward, a full bathroom renovation the same weekend is not. If your landlord is habitually unresponsive, our guide on [getting your landlord to fix things](/posts/getting-landlord-to-fix-things-nz/) covers the escalation path, and [what happens when a property manager goes quiet](/posts/property-manager-not-responding-nz/) covers the agency version of the same problem.

## The Insurance Trap

Here's what catches renters out, and there's no nice way to put it.

**Your landlord's insurance does not cover your belongings.** Their policy covers the building. Your bed, your laptop, your clothes, the contents of your fridge — those are only covered if *you* hold contents insurance.

There is nothing in the Residential Tenancies Act that shifts the cost of your possessions onto the landlord after a flood or a fire that wasn't their fault. If the damage was caused by something they neglected — a leak they'd been told about for months and ignored — that's a different argument and one worth taking to the Tribunal. But a storm is a storm.

Some contents policies also cover temporary accommodation while you're displaced. Whether yours does, and for how much, is a question for your insurer rather than something to assume — but it's the first call to make if you're suddenly paying for somewhere to sleep.

> **Warning:** If you don't have contents insurance, the week after a flood is the week you find out what that decision cost. This is the one thing in this article worth acting on *before* anything goes wrong.

## Who Pays for Somewhere to Sleep?

The hard answer, and [Tenancy Services states it directly](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/maintenance-and-inspections/damage-and-repairs/what-to-do-after-a-natural-disaster/): **the landlord is not obligated to provide alternative housing.** They may be able to help you find another property, but that's assistance, not an obligation.

That's a real gap and it's worth understanding before you're standing in it. The Act's remedy is financial and it's about the tenancy — you stop paying for an unlivable house, and you can walk away on two days' notice. It isn't a right to be housed at the landlord's expense.

Tenancy Services points displaced tenants to three places:

| Where | When it applies |
|---|---|
| **Civil defence emergency accommodation** | During and after a declared emergency |
| **Temporary Accommodation Service** | Longer displacement following a significant event |
| **Emergency housing, Ministry of Social Development** | Where you have nowhere to stay and can't afford it |

Your own contents insurance may also cover temporary accommodation — see above. Start with the insurer, because that call is quick and the answer determines whether you need the rest.

> **Tip:** If your landlord does offer alternative accommodation, get the terms in writing — specifically whether you're still paying rent, and what happens to your tenancy at the original address. A verbal arrangement made in a crisis is exactly the kind that gets remembered differently later.

## What About Damp and Mould After the Water Goes?

A flooded house that gets dried badly becomes a mouldy house three months later, and that's a different legal problem with a different answer — the [healthy homes standards](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/healthy-homes/) and the landlord's ongoing maintenance obligations rather than section 59.

## Your Position, Summarised

If your rental is genuinely uninhabitable:

- The **rent abates** — you don't pay for what you can't live in
- You can end the tenancy on **2 days' notice**, fixed term or not
- Your landlord needs **7 days**
- **Partial damage** means partial abatement, and the Tribunal can end it if reinstatement is unreasonable
- **Urgent repairs** can be arranged by you and billed back, if you tried to reach them first
- **Your contents are your problem** unless you're insured

Get the official notice, photograph everything, and put every conversation in writing. The law is on your side here more than in almost any other tenancy situation — but only as far as you can prove what happened.
