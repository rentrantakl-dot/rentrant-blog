A For Sale sign goes up outside the house you live in, and the ground shifts a bit. Where are you going to live? Do you have to start looking now? What happens to your bond?

Start with the reassuring part, because it's the most important thing in this guide: **a sale does not automatically end your tenancy.** In New Zealand, the property changes hands with you in it unless someone properly ends the tenancy. Plenty of renters start packing when they didn't need to.

There are two ways this goes.

---

## Scenario One: The Buyer Keeps You On

A buyer who wants a tenanted investment property has no reason to empty it first — so this outcome is worth ruling in before you assume the worst.

If the buyer is happy to continue the tenancy, per [Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/ending-a-tenancy/change-of-landlord-or-tenant/selling-a-rental-property/):

- The buyer **becomes your new landlord**
- The tenancy continues on the **same terms and conditions** — Tenancy Services puts it as *"the terms and conditions in the tenancy agreement are not affected by the sale"*
- **That includes the rules around rent increases.** A new owner doesn't get a fresh start on the 12-month clock
- You do **not** sign a new tenancy agreement
- The landlord details on the agreement get updated, as do the details on the bond record

That's it. Same rent, same agreement, same rights, new name on the emails.

The rent-increase point is the one most worth holding onto. A change of ownership is not a reset — if your last increase was four months ago, the next one is still eight months away, whoever owns the place.

> **Key point:** Tenancy Services is clear that you don't need to sign a new tenancy agreement when a property sells. So if a new owner presents you with one, understand what's happening — that's not paperwork housekeeping, it's a new set of terms. Ask what's changed from the agreement you already have before you sign anything.

## Scenario Two: The Buyer Wants It Empty

If the sale is conditional on vacant possession, the tenancy has to be properly ended — and how depends entirely on what kind of tenancy you have.

### If you're on a periodic tenancy

The landlord must give you **at least 42 days' written notice** to end the tenancy so the property is vacant for the new owner.

Forty-two days is the shortened notice period that applies to specific grounds including sale with vacant possession — as opposed to the 90 days required when a landlord ends a periodic tenancy without cause. Our guide to [90-day notice and your options](/posts/90-day-notice-your-rights-and-options/) covers the difference and what a valid notice has to contain.

The notice must be in writing. A conversation at the letterbox is not notice.

### If you're on a fixed term

This is the one worth knowing, and Tenancy Services is unambiguous: **the landlord cannot give notice to end the tenancy early just because the property has sold.**

The buyer takes the property with your fixed term attached, and becomes your landlord for whatever remains of it. If you signed a twelve-month fixed term in March and the place sells in July, you have until the following March — regardless of what the new owner had planned.

There are two legitimate ways around it, and neither is a notice. The parties can **agree** to end it early. Or the landlord can arrange a **later settlement date** with the buyer, so the tenancy runs its course first.

So if you're asked to leave early, that's a request. You can agree if it suits you, and there's nothing wrong with negotiating something in return for the disruption — but you're entitled to say no.

## Viewings: You Have More Say Than You Think

This is where the sale actually intrudes on your life, and where most of the friction happens.

Viewings need **your permission**, and [Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/maintenance-and-inspections/access/) states it about as plainly as it can be stated:

> **Key point:** *"Landlords must ask permission from their tenants to have any professionals or potential buyers enter the property. Landlords cannot simply give notice to their tenants if they want to have an open home or show potential buyers through the house."*

You can't unreasonably refuse — but permission is not a rubber stamp, and it means you get a say in the conditions: what times, how often, and whether you're present.

We've covered this in detail in [can my landlord show my flat while I'm still living there](/briefs/viewings-during-notice-period-nz/), which is worth reading before the first one is scheduled.

The practical move is to get ahead of it. Propose the arrangement yourself, in writing, before the agent proposes theirs:

> **Template:**
>
> > Hi [Name],
> >
> > Happy to accommodate viewings for the sale. To make it workable at my end, can we agree on the following:
> >
> > - Viewings on [days], between [times]
> > - Maximum [number] per week
> > - At least 24 hours' notice of each one
> > - I'd like to be present / I'm happy for you to let yourself in with notice
> >
> > Let me know if that works and I'll make sure the place is tidy for them.
> >
> > Cheers,
> > [Your name]

A tenant who proposes a reasonable schedule in writing is in a far stronger position than one who starts refusing viewings after the third unannounced Saturday.

You're not obliged to stage the house, keep it show-home tidy, or bake anything. Reasonable tidiness is your normal obligation and it doesn't increase because someone's trying to sell.

## Your Bond

The bond doesn't get refunded and re-paid when a property sells. It stays lodged with Tenancy Services, and the **old landlord's interest in it passes to the new landlord**.

Both landlords have to send Tenancy Services a completed change of landlord or agent form.

One detail worth knowing: the outgoing landlord **cannot claim against the bond after settlement** unless they do so before that date. So if there's a dispute brewing about the state of the property, the sale imposes a deadline on their side of it.

Your job is to check it actually happened:

- [ ] **Confirm the bond record shows the new landlord** — contact Tenancy Services directly if you're unsure
- [ ] **Get the new landlord's full contact details in writing**, including an address for service
- [ ] **Keep your original tenancy agreement** — it's still the agreement that governs you
- [ ] **Keep your entry condition report** from when you moved in — this is what you'll be measured against at the end, not the state the new owner found the place in

That last one matters more than anything else on the list. A new landlord who never saw the property when you moved in may form views about its condition that your original report quietly demolishes. Our guide to [your bond rights](/posts/your-bond-rights-nz/) covers the end-of-tenancy process, and [fair wear and tear](/posts/fair-wear-and-tear-nz/) covers what can and can't be deducted.

## Your Rights Don't Pause During a Sale

This is where standards slip, and it's worth being firm about.

A property being on the market changes nothing about your landlord's ongoing obligations. While the sale is happening, they still have to:

- **Do the repairs.** A broken heat pump doesn't get to wait until settlement. See [getting your landlord to fix things](/posts/getting-landlord-to-fix-things-nz/)
- **Meet the healthy homes standards.** Every private rental in New Zealand has had to comply since 1 July 2025, and a pending sale isn't an exemption — our [healthy homes compliance guide](/posts/is-your-auckland-rental-healthy-homes-compliant/) covers how to check
- **Give proper notice for entry.** Inspections still need the normal notice; viewings still need your consent. Neither becomes a free pass because there's an agent involved. See [rental inspections](/posts/rental-inspections-nz/)
- **Leave you in quiet enjoyment** of the property between viewings

The common friction point is repairs. A landlord about to sell has an obvious incentive to defer anything that costs money, and "the new owner can sort that out" is a sentence a lot of tenants hear during a sale campaign.

It isn't a valid answer. The obligation sits with whoever is your landlord at the time, and it transfers with the property rather than lapsing on the way. If a repair request is being parked until settlement, put it in writing, keep the reply, and escalate on the normal path.

> **Key point:** Photograph the property's condition when the sale campaign starts, especially if any staging, painting or gardening is done for the marketing. Work carried out to make a house sell can quietly change what "the condition you received it in" looks like — and you want your own record of it, not just theirs.

---

## If the Timing Feels Suspicious

One narrow but real scenario: if the sale process kicked off shortly after you complained about something — a repair, a healthy homes issue, an inspection — the timing is worth noting.

Retaliatory action is unlawful, and our guide to [retaliatory termination](/posts/retaliatory-termination-nz/) covers what has to be shown. Selling a property is an entirely legitimate thing for an owner to do, so this is not a card to play lightly. But if a 42-day notice lands three weeks after you raised a mould problem in writing, keep the correspondence.

## Where You Actually Stand

| Your situation | What happens |
|---|---|
| Buyer keeps the tenancy | Continues on identical terms, no new agreement, new landlord's name on the bond record |
| Periodic, buyer wants it empty | At least **42 days' written notice** |
| Fixed term | Generally can't be ended early — **the sale doesn't break your fixed term** |
| Viewings | Need your consent; you can set reasonable conditions |
| Bond | Stays lodged; interest passes to the new landlord |

A sale is disruptive and often stressful, but it's rarely the emergency it feels like on the day the sign goes up. Work out which of those rows you're in, get the dates in writing, and go from there.
